---
title: "Things I Wish I Knew When I Started Coding"
date: "2025-09-20"
---

Starting a journey in software development can feel like drinking from a firehose. There's an endless sea of languages, frameworks, and tools to learn, and it's easy to feel overwhelmed. Over time, every developer learns that some of the things that once caused so much stress weren't as important as they seemed. Here are a few key lessons I wish I'd understood earlier.

### You Don't Need to Know Everything
One of the biggest myths junior developers believe is that they need to master every aspect of a language or technology. The reality is, real-world development is an open-book test. Experienced developers are constantly Googling, checking documentation, and looking up solutions. The key skill isn't memorization; it's understanding core concepts, recognizing patterns, and knowing how to find the right information. Focus on problem-solving, not on becoming a walking encyclopedia.

### Learn How to Learn
Passively watching tutorials can give you a false sense of competence. True skill is built through active application. Instead of watching another 10-hour course, spend that time building a small project. The most valuable learning happens when you're struggling to fix a bug, experimenting with a new feature, or applying what you've just learned to a real problem. Embrace the struggle—it's where growth happens.

### Perfection Is a Trap
Chasing the "perfect" piece of code is a common trap that slows down progress and kills momentum. It's far more important to build, ship, and iterate. Your first version might be "ugly" or inefficient, but it works. Getting something functional out the door provides feedback and a foundation to improve upon. Learning is an iterative process; don't wait for everything to be ideal before you move forward.

### You’ll Never Feel "Ready"
If you wait until you feel completely ready to apply for a job, contribute to a project, or tackle a new challenge, you'll be waiting forever. Confidence is a byproduct of action, not a prerequisite for it. Self-doubt and imposter syndrome are incredibly common, even among senior developers. The secret is to jump in anyway. You'll learn and adapt as you go.

### Problem-Solving Is the Real Skill
At its heart, software development is about one thing: solving problems. The specific syntax of a language or the features of a framework are just the tools you use to do it. The most crucial skills are breaking down a large, complex problem into smaller, manageable pieces and having the relentless curiosity to debug issues systematically. Cultivate your thinking process, and the coding will follow.

### Nobody Cares About Your Code—Only the Results
It's easy to get caught up in writing the most elegant, clever, or "correct" code. While code quality is important for maintainability, employers and clients ultimately care about one thing: a working product that delivers value. The focus should always be on achieving the desired outcome. Don't let obsession over implementation details distract you from the primary goal of making something that works and helps people.

### Final Advice
Embrace imperfection and keep starting, even when you feel scared or unprepared. Focus on solving problems, build things consistently, and don't forget to prioritize your health and well-being. The journey of a developer is a marathon, not a sprint, and internalizing these lessons can make it a lot less stressful and much more fulfilling.
